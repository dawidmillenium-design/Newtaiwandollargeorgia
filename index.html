The line that started it all

A coffee. One Georgian lari. A tap of the card, a beep, and the humid Black Sea air. Then, later that night, the banking app showed this:

Płatność kartą −1,00 TWN
georgian vending group, Tbilisi · Today
Dopasowana Mastercard debit ····••••

TWN. "New Taiwan Dollar." In Georgia. For a coffee drunk with one's own hands, in a country entered legally two weeks earlier, on a card that never left the wallet. The first instinct was the classic traveller's dread: a cloned card feeding through a terminal in Taipei. The amount and location looked consistent with my coffee purchase, but the unexpected code still warranted a check with my card issuer.

So what is "TWN"?

Here is the punchline: TWN is not a currency. It does not appear anywhere in ISO 4217 — the international standard for currency codes. It exists in no central bank's ledger. (It is a real code — just not for money: TWN is Taiwan's ISO 3166 country code, which is exactly the kind of thing a buggy currency table confuses.) The actual cast of characters:

The FX sanity check

The number next to the ghost is honest: 1,00 — the lari price, digit for digit. One GEL ≈ 1.4–1.5 zł ≈ 11–12 TWD. A genuine 1-TWD charge would be about 0.12 zł — pocket change, not a coffee. The matching amount suggests a display or mapping problem, but it does not identify which part of the payment chain produced the label. Only the card issuer or processor can confirm how the charge was authorized and settled.

What the records do and do not show

Travel records place me in Georgia around the time of the coffee purchase. They help explain the context, but they cannot by themselves authenticate the card transaction or rule out unauthorized use:

The merchant description, amount, and my own receipt seem consistent with a local purchase. Those observations do not establish the source of the TWN label or eliminate every possibility of an unauthorized charge.

Assessment: the display code is unexplained. I would ask the issuing bank to verify the merchant, authorization currency, settlement currency, and amount before drawing a firm conclusion.

Where the code might have changed

These are possible explanations, not findings from the bank or processor.

🖥️

Terminal misconfiguration

The terminal could have sent an unexpected code, but its configuration has not been inspected.

🔀

Acquirer / processor mapping bug

A processor or issuer mapping error is another possibility; the transaction records are needed to locate it.

📋

ISO non-compliance

TWN is a country code, while TWD and GEL are currency codes. The appearance of TWN does not show where the mismatch occurred.

🧾

Three layers, zero agreement

The receipt, merchant descriptor, and app display differ. Keep all three records and ask the issuer to reconcile them.

What to do when your statement grows a ghost

  1. Contact the issuing bank promptly if you do not recognize a charge. Ask whether to lock or replace the card while it investigates. Mastercard advises contacting the financial institution immediately when unauthorized use is suspected.
  2. Keep the receipt (the one showing 1 GEL) until the line posts correctly.
  3. Check the posted amount and currency. A pending display may differ from the final statement; ask the issuer to explain any mismatch.
  4. Contact the bank with evidence: the screenshot of the "−1,00 TWN" line, the 1 GEL receipt, and any relevant travel details requested through the bank's secure channel.
  5. Ask for a written explanation: which currency was authorized, which was settled, what exchange rate was used, and why the app displayed TWN.
Honest caveat: ink can't be authenticated from a photo, and two small quirks deserve a glance only if a third party ever challenges the documents — the Thai oval's airport name looks compressed ("SUVAR…BHUMI", usually just curvature/ink bleed), and the Georgian rectangle's star-circle around "GE" superficially resembles an EU motif (Georgia is not EU). Neither authenticates the bank transaction or resolves the display code.

What the ghost teaches

We hand our trust to a chain of boxes we cannot see: terminal → acquirer → network → issuer → app, each one re-typing what the last one said, each one allowed a small mistake that compounds into a headline like "New Taiwan Dollar" on a Georgian coffee. The drama isn't that money moved somewhere it shouldn't. The drama is that the label detached from the thing. The coffee and travel records are my observations; the three-letter display code still needs an explanation from the issuer.

If your statement shows an unfamiliar currency or a charge you cannot identify, preserve the receipt and contact your card issuer promptly. The issuer can confirm the transaction details and advise whether to lock the card.
