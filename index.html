The Ghost Currency Code — Hub & Overview

The currency that never was. New Taiwan Dollar → a firmware typo
The story of a 1 GEL coffee in Batumi, Georgia, haunted by a phantom three-letter code.

The line that started it all

A coffee. One Georgian lari. A tap of the card, a beep, and the humid Black Sea air. Then, later that night, the banking app showed this:

Płatność kartą −1,00 TWN
georgian vending group, Tbilisi · Today
Dopasowana Mastercard debit ····2673

TWN. "New Taiwan Dollar." In Georgia. For a coffee drunk with one's own hands, in a country entered legally two weeks earlier, on a card that never left the wallet. The first instinct was the classic traveller's dread: a cloned card feeding through a terminal in Taipei. The second instinct — after looking at the numbers and the passport stamps — was warmer and stranger: nobody cloned anything. The machine simply lied about what money is.

So what is "TWN"?

Here is the punchline: TWN is not a currency. It does not appear anywhere in ISO 4217 — the international standard for currency codes. It exists in no central bank's ledger. (It is a real code — just not for money: TWN is Taiwan's ISO 3166 country code, which is exactly the kind of thing a buggy currency table confuses.) The actual cast of characters:

The FX sanity check

The number next to the ghost is honest: 1,00 — the lari price, digit for digit. One GEL ≈ 1.4–1.5 zł ≈ 11–12 TWD. A genuine 1-TWD charge would be about 0.12 zł — pocket change, not a coffee. So "1,00 TWN" can only be the raw GEL figure wearing a stolen, invalid label. The amount field survived the journey intact; the currency field got mangled somewhere between the vending terminal, the Georgian acquirer in Tbilisi, and the bank's parser.

Why it isn't fraud — the ink says so

Fraud has a geography problem, and this case resolves it for free. The passport stamps place the cardholder — and the card — physically in Georgia:

  • 18 Jul 2019
    Old passport ES1421811 issued — later voided with a cancellation punch-hole through its MRZ.
  • 20 Jul 2026
    New passport FL0263070 issued by the Consul of Poland in Bangkok (valid to 2036) — proof of mid-2026 residence in Thailand.
  • 14 Sep 2026
    🛫 Suvarnabhumi Airport — oval blue Thai IMMIGRATION stamp: DEPARTED.
  • 15 Sep 2026
    ✈️ BATUMI · GE · 15 09 2026 · 0387 — rectangular grey entry stamp with the five-cross icon: arrival by air at Batumi International.
  • 29 Sep 2026
    ☕ The 1 GEL coffee. Timestamped "Today", merchant descriptor georgian vending group. Two weeks deep inside Georgia.
  • Same moment
    👻 The bank line is born reading −1,00 TWN — a currency that does not exist, in a country that does.

A cloned card swiped in Taiwan would carry a Taiwanese merchant name and a TWD amount unrelated to any latte. It would not greet you with "Hello, Dawid!" on your own logged-in app, matching DAVID LISOWSKI on the data page, on a card ending 2673 you personally activated. And the Tbilisi-vs-Batumi descriptor mismatch? Normal noise: most Georgian acquirers and vending operators are registered in the capital, so the statement prints the processor's home city while the terminal sits on a Batumi boulevard.

Verdict: local transaction, bad metadata. You paid 1 GEL at a Batumi merchant with your own card while legally inside Georgia. No evidence of skimming or cloning — just a broken currency tag on an otherwise correct amount.

How a ghost gets into the machine

🖥️

Terminal misconfiguration

A cheap vending terminal ships with a corrupted currency table in its firmware — a hole shaped like TWN where GEL should be.

🔀

Acquirer / processor mapping bug

Somewhere between the Georgian terminal and the issuing bank's parser, the GEL field got re-tagged with garbage during message translation.

📋

ISO non-compliance

Someone used "TWN" — Taiwan's ISO 3166 country code — in a slot that expects an ISO 4217 currency code. Country ≠ currency.

🧾

Three layers, zero agreement

Receipt said ALLIANCE PALACA, descriptor said "georgian vending group, Tbilisi", currency said "TWN". The payment stack is a Rorschach test — trust the ink, not the metadata.

What to do when your statement grows a ghost

  1. Don't panic-block the card. The geography and the amount both point to a genuine local sale. Freezing a good card over a firmware typo buys you a week in support purgatory.
  2. Keep the receipt (the one showing 1 GEL) until the line posts correctly.
  3. Watch the settled zł amount. If the back-office really tried to convert "1.00 TWN", you'll see either a comically tiny posting (~0.1–0.2 zł) or the item held/rejected — both fixable, and both prove the display is wrong.
  4. Contact the bank with evidence: the screenshot of the "−1,00 TWN" line, the 1 GEL receipt, and optionally the Batumi entry stamp (15 09 2026) as proof of life in Georgia, not Taiwan.
  5. Request, in writing: relabel the currency code to GEL and confirm the FX conversion actually used. A non-existent ISO code breaks reconciliation and can trip false fraud flags later.
Honest caveat: ink can't be authenticated from a photo, and two small quirks deserve a glance only if a third party ever challenges the documents — the Thai oval's airport name looks compressed ("SUVAR…BHUMI", usually just curvature/ink bleed), and the Georgian rectangle's star-circle around "GE" superficially resembles an EU motif (Georgia is not EU). Neither affects the verdict above.

What the ghost teaches

We hand our trust to a chain of boxes we cannot see: terminal → acquirer → network → issuer → app, each one re-typing what the last one said, each one allowed a small mistake that compounds into a headline like "New Taiwan Dollar" on a Georgian coffee. The drama isn't that money moved somewhere it shouldn't. The drama is that the label detached from the thing. The lari was real, the coffee was real, the stamps were real; only the three letters were a hallucination.

So the next time your statement shows a currency you've never heard of, before you reach for the "block card" button — reach for your passport instead. Sometimes the scariest line on the screen is just a machine that forgot what world it lives in, and the ink, patient and boring, is the only one telling the truth.
